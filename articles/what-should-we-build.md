Product managers and experience designers have a long-running argument about who decides what gets built. It's usually had as a turf war, between two jobs with different instincts.

{% timeline "what-should-we-build" %}
Their histories suggest something odder. For a hundred years the two have kept borrowing each other's methods. Marketing's focus group and usability's think-aloud both sit people down and ask them. Customer development rediscovers user research and calls it getting out of the building. Business schools teach design thinking forty years after Bruce Archer and Herbert Simon wrote it down.

Two disciplines with different roots, different evidence and different bosses keep reinventing each other. Why?

As you read, watch for the people the work is for: where they stand, and how often anyone asks them. The first two cards are a good place to start. They're two studies of the same work in 1911, one in each lane.

---

Read back, the two lanes look less like rivals than like two desks in the same office. Both are, in their way, Taylor's planning department: the brain work, moved off the shop floor and into a room of its own. Product plans for the business and experience plans for the person, but both plan on someone else's behalf. That's why they keep borrowing from each other. It's the same department.

They haven't held the job in the same way. Experience design has spent a century arguing with itself about whether the department should exist at all: Lewin's research with people rather than on them, Fals-Borda's participatory action research, the Scandinavian unions with a say in their own tools, Suchman's users who knew more than the plan, and now humanity-centred and systemic design. Product has mostly occupied the department, and got very good at running it.

The argument has never been settled. It's about to get much harder to avoid.
{% endtimeline %}

## Who gets to ask

The distance between the people who plan and the people they plan for has been shrinking for a long time. In 1969 Herbert Simon said that everyone designs who tries to change a situation into a better one. Ten years later VisiCalc let accountants and managers build their own financial models, without asking a programmer and without waiting for one. In 2015 Ezio Manzini asked what expert designers are for when everybody designs, and answered that they should help everyone else do it well.

Generative AI makes that practical in a way it never was. Someone with a problem can describe a tool and be using it the same afternoon. Citizen science has already shown how much people can find out when they're given the means. The participatory tradition's oldest aim, that the people affected by a system help shape it, is closer than it has ever been.

There's already a working example. From 2013, under the hashtag #WeAreNotWaiting, people with type 1 diabetes and their families started building the tools the device industry hadn't. By 2015 some were running artificial pancreas systems they had built themselves, and sharing the designs openly as OpenAPS. Nobody planned it for them. They planned it, built it and tested it on themselves, and the people who knew most about living with the condition were the ones deciding what to build.

The same technology can close the gap from the other side. Taylor's time study needed a man with a stopwatch to watch the worker. Software doesn't. In 2019 The Verge reported on documents from a US labour case describing how Amazon tracked each warehouse worker's productivity, including “time off task”, and generated warnings and terminations without input from supervisors. Amazon said supervisors could override the system. Either way, it's Taylor's stopwatch without the timekeeper, at a scale he never imagined. The worker is measured all the time, and asked nothing.

Those two stories rest on much the same kind of technology. In one, the people the work is for decide what gets built. In the other, they're measured by it more closely than ever. Nothing in the technology settles which of those we get more of. That depends on what gets built on top of it, who it's built for and who's asked along the way. AI can abolish the planning department or automate it, and which way it goes is a product and design decision, not a technical one.

The decisions are ordinary ones, the kind both lanes make every week. Whether a tool shows its workings or hides them. Whether the people using it can change what it does. What it measures, who sees the measurements and what happens next. Whether there's a person to appeal to. None of these is decided by a model. Each is decided by whoever specifies the product, and whoever they think to ask.

That isn't the same as saying everyone is now a builder. OpenAPS worked because its builders had deep technical skill, and put safety limits and documentation at the centre of the project. Building gets cheaper; knowing what's safe, sound and worth building doesn't. But the tools, platforms and defaults being built now will shape who gets to decide for years to come.

My guess is that each lane's future grows from its own past. Product has always asked what's worth building for the business. It becomes the steward of the conditions people build in: the platforms, constraints and guardrails that make building for yourself safe and worth doing. That's Manzini's expert design, in support of diffuse design. Experience has always asked what's worth building for people. It becomes the arbiter of whose value counts, which is the work humanity-centred and systemic design have already started: who else is affected, and who isn't in the room.

OpenAPS shows why both are needed. Someone has to check the system is safe, which is a question of conditions: what the software is allowed to do, how it fails and what happens when a sensor drops out. Someone has to ask who it's safe for, which is a question of people: those without the skills, time or money to build their own, and those a self-built system leaves out. Neither question is answered by the builder alone. Neither is answered by a planning department deciding on everyone's behalf. Both need people whose job is to think about everyone who isn't building, and that is what the two lanes have been practising, separately, for a hundred years.

What should we build? For a century, the answer has depended on who ‘we’ meant. It still does.
